How Often to Follow Up on a Quote: A Simple Schedule

By Steve Heienickle, founder of Agla Consulting · November 11, 2026

You send a quote, and then nothing. No yes, no no, just silence. Most service businesses handle that silence one of two ways: they chase too hard, or they wait for the customer to call and never hear back.

A quiet quote usually isn’t a “no.” Often the customer got busy, is comparing options, or simply forgot. A short, predictable schedule keeps you in the conversation without nagging. This article gives you one you can start using today, plus messages you can copy.

A note on statistics: you may have seen “80% of sales need five follow-ups” repeated everywhere. We left it out because we could not find a reliable study behind it. What follows is a practical rule of thumb, not a research finding. Adjust it to your own customers and track what works.

Why quotes go quiet

  • The customer got busy. A quote is rarely the most urgent thing in their week.
  • They are comparing. Two or three other companies may be quoting the same job.
  • Something in the quote is unclear. A price, a scope line or a date raised a question they did not want to ask.
  • Nobody checked back. The quote went out, the crew got busy, and it was forgotten on both sides.

A simple five-touch schedule

Count days from the day the quote is sent. Stop the moment the customer replies, whether they say yes, no or “not yet.”

WhenWhat to sendGoal
Day 0The quote itself, the same day as the request if you can. Say what happens next and how long the price is good for.Show you are organized and responsive
Day 2A short check: “Did the quote come through? Any questions?”Confirm it arrived and invite questions
Day 5Something useful: answer a common question, offer to adjust the scope or share a photo of a similar finished job.Give a reason to reply
Day 10A direct, easy question: “Is this still on your list?”Get a clear yes, no or later
Day 21A polite close-out: “I’ll close this quote for now. Reach out anytime.”End cleanly and leave the door open

After day 21, stop. If you would like to stay in touch, add the customer to a light seasonal reminder once or twice a year instead of continuing to chase the quote.

Adjust the timing to the job

One schedule does not fit every job. Use these as starting points and change them when your own results tell you to.

Type of jobPace
Urgent or small (a repair, a one-visit job)Faster: check back the next day and again on day 3, since these decisions are made quickly.
Typical home-service jobThe schedule above.
Large or planned project (a remodel, a seasonal install)Slower and longer: roughly weekly for four to six weeks, with more useful content and fewer nudges.

Four messages you can copy

Keep them short, friendly and written in your own voice. Replace the details in brackets.

Day 2: the check-in

Hi [Name], I wanted to make sure the quote for [job] came through. If anything is unclear, I’m happy to walk through it or adjust it. Thanks, [Your name]

Day 5: the useful one

Hi [Name], a question we hear a lot on [job type] is [common question]. The short answer is [answer]. If it would help to change the scope or the timing, just tell me what matters most. [Your name]

Day 10: the direct question

Hi [Name], is the [job] still on your list? A quick “yes,” “not right now” or “went another way” is all I need, and any of them is fine. [Your name]

Day 21: the close-out

Hi [Name], I’m going to close out the quote for [job] for now. If your plans change, reply to this message and I’ll pick it back up. Thanks for considering us. [Your name]

Five rules that keep it professional

  1. Stop when they reply. A follow-up that arrives after the customer has answered feels careless.
  2. Give each quote one owner. If it is everyone’s job, it is no one’s.
  3. Log every touch. Record each follow-up in your CRM or a shared sheet so nobody sends the same message twice.
  4. Honor opt-outs. If someone asks you to stop, stop right away and mark it in your records. If you text customers, only do so when they have agreed to it.
  5. Only state a deadline if it is real. A genuine expiry date helps. An invented one damages trust.
