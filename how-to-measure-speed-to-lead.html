How to Measure Your Speed to Lead in 30 Minutes

By Steve Heienickle, founder of Agla Consulting · November 11, 2026

Almost every owner believes they reply to new leads quickly. Very few have timed it. The gap between what you think happens and what the clock says is usually the most useful number in your business.

This guide shows you how to measure it with a spreadsheet, no new software required. It builds on our article on why slow lead follow-up costs service businesses jobs, which explains why the number matters. Here we focus on how to get it.

What “speed to lead” means

Speed to lead is the time between when an inquiry arrives and when a real person (or an approved system) first replies in a way that moves things forward.

Two details matter. First, the clock starts when the inquiry arrives, not when someone happens to see it. Second, an automatic “we got your message” email is useful, but it does not count as the first real reply. Track it separately if you like.

Five steps

1. Pick a window

Use the last 30 days, or the last 30 leads if you get fewer. Include every source: website form, email, social media messages and chat. Leave out nothing, especially the ones you lost.

2. Find two times for each lead

You need when it arrived and when your first real reply went out.

  • Arrived: the time on the form notification email, the “created” time in your CRM, or the timestamp on the social or chat message.
  • First reply: the sent time in your email, the time of your message in the social inbox or chat, or the time of the first logged contact in your CRM.

If your CRM does not capture these times, rebuild them from your inbox. It is tedious the first time, and that tedium is a finding in itself.

3. Put them in a sheet

Create one row per lead with these columns: Lead, Source, Arrived, First reply, Minutes. Here is an example with made-up leads:

AWebsite formMon 8:15 AMMon 8:40 AM25
BSocial messageMon 7:30 PMTue 8:10 AM760

DEmailWed 10:00 AMWed 11:30 AM90

4. Let the spreadsheet do the math

These formulas work in Excel and Google Sheets. Format the Arrived and First reply columns as date and time, and use real dates, not just the day of the week.

  • Minutes for one lead: =(D2-C2)*1440
  • Typical response (median): =MEDIAN(E2:E31)
  • Share answered within an hour: =COUNTIF(E2:E31,"<=60")/COUNT(E2:E31)

In the example, the median is 57.5 minutes and half the leads got a reply within an hour. The average would be about 498 minutes, pulled up by one weekend lead. That is why we recommend the median for the typical case and the “within an hour” share for the whole picture.

5. Slice it three ways

  • By source. Does one channel, such as social messages, lag behind the others?
  • By time. Compare weekday business hours with evenings and weekends. The weekend lead in the example waited almost a day and a half.
  • By person. If different people reply, see who is fast and what they do differently.

A shortcut: send yourself test leads

If your records are thin, send three test inquiries through your website form from a personal email address: one during the day, one in the evening and one on a weekend. Write down when each was sent and when a real reply arrives. Three data points will not be statistically perfect, but they will show you the shape of the problem quickly.

Five common traps

  1. Counting auto-replies. Useful, but not the same as a person responding.
  2. Using the average. One slow weekend lead can hide good performance, or the reverse. Use the median.
  3. Only measuring leads you won. The ones you lost are often the slow ones.
  4. Starting the clock too late. Time from when someone saw the lead is not time from when it arrived.
  5. Measuring only business hours. It hides the nights and weekends where many leads are lost. Track clock time and business-hours time separately.

What a good number looks like

We cannot give you a universal target, because it depends on your business and your customers. A sensible starting goal for most service businesses is a real reply within an hour on weekdays and an immediate acknowledgment after hours, with a real reply first thing the next morning. The research we covered in the earlier article points the same direction: the sooner the better. Pick a target you can actually hit, then tighten it as you improve.

What to do with the number

Look at your slowest category first, whether that is evenings, weekends or one source, and fix that gap before anything else. Then repeat the measurement in 30 days. If the target is hard to hit with the team you have, that is the part software can take over: taking in each inquiry, checking it, and sending a reply within minutes, with a person approving until you trust it.
