Why Slow Lead Follow-Up Costs Service Businesses Jobs

By Steve Heienickle, founder of Agla Consulting · November 11, 2026

A homeowner fills out your quote form on a Saturday night. Your crew is done for the day and the office is closed, so the request sits in an inbox until Monday. By then, that homeowner has often already heard from someone else.

Most service business owners know this is a problem. Few have measured it. This article covers what the research says about response speed, why service businesses lose leads in particular places, and six things you can do this week without buying anything.

What the research shows

The most cited study on this comes from Harvard Business Review (opens in a new tab). Researchers followed 1.25 million sales leads sent to 42 U.S. companies, 29 selling to consumers and 13 selling to businesses.

  • Companies that tried to contact a lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to have a meaningful conversation with a decision maker as companies that waited even one more hour.
  • Compared with companies that waited 24 hours or longer, the gap was more than 60 times.

The article also reports an audit in which a web inquiry was sent to 2,241 U.S. companies and the replies were timed. Only 37% responded within an hour. About 24% took more than a day, and 23% never responded at all. Among the companies that replied within 30 days, the average wait was 42 hours.

A fair caution: the study was not limited to home services and it dates from 2011, so treat the exact multiples as a direction, not a promise. The direction is what matters. The longer an inquiry waits, the less likely it becomes a conversation, and most businesses are slower than they think.

Why service businesses lose leads in particular places

In a service business, the people who would reply are the same people doing the work. That creates a few predictable leaks:

  • The busy-owner gap. The owner or estimator is on a job site or in a truck. Replies wait for a quiet moment that may not come until evening.
  • Nights and weekends. Many people request quotes after work or on a Saturday, exactly when nobody is at a desk.
  • Too many inboxes. Website forms, email, social media messages and chat each land in a different place, and one of them is always checked last.
  • Messy records. A request with no house number, a mistyped phone number or a duplicate of an existing customer slows everyone down before the first reply.
  • No one owns the follow-up. A quote goes out and nobody checks back. If the customer says nothing, the job quietly dies.

Six things you can do this week

  1. Time yourself. Submit your own quote form on a weeknight and on a weekend, and note when the first reply arrives. Most owners have never done this, and the answer is usually a surprise.
  2. Acknowledge instantly. Even a simple automatic email that confirms the request and says when they will hear back is better than silence. It sets an expectation while you work on the real answer.
  3. Put every lead source in one place. Forms, email, social messages and chat should all end up in the same list, with the source recorded so you can see which marketing works.
  4. Give each lead one named owner and a backup. “The office” is not an owner. A person, plus someone who covers when they are out, is.
  5. Set a follow-up schedule for quotes. For example, a friendly check-in two days after the quote and another a week later, stopping the moment the customer answers.
  6. Clean your customer list once a month. Merge duplicates, fix missing phone numbers and addresses, and remove old leads so reports reflect reality.
